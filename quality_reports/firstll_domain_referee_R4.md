# Domain Referee Report
**Date:** 2026-09-06
**Paper:** The First Break: Career Effects of Initial Access Shocks in Tournament Labor Markets
**Field:** Labor economics (opportunity shocks, career trajectories); sports as laboratory
**Calibrated to:** JHR / AEJ:Applied (dual target)
**Recommendation:** Major Revisions (upper end — one focused revision away from Minor)
**Overall Score:** 76/100

## Summary

The paper delivers the first credibly causal estimates of a within-career opportunity shock in a tournament labor market by exploiting the Grand Slam lucky-loser lottery among LL-naive players and extending to non-GS events with a combinatorially derived control function. The findings are sharp and honestly hedged: ATP access effects at 4–12 weeks, WTA short-horizon gains, an ability channel that is largely null on the ATP tour and marginally positive on the WTA tour, and a self-limiting cascade that fades within 52 weeks. The identification is clean and the mechanism decomposition (access vs. ability), the mechanical decomposition of the "first-break" claim, and the news verification are the kind of work I want to see. My concerns are not with execution but with three interpretive claims that carry a lot of weight in the manuscript: (i) whether "first break" is a behavioral phenomenon or a mechanical diminishing-returns artifact, (ii) whether the paper is differentiated from Pallais (2014), which is a much closer analog than Oyer/Oreopoulos, and (iii) whether the headline $100K figure is a causal cascade or a mechanical conditional-on-advancement immediate-event prize.

## Major Comments

**1. The "first-break" interpretation blurs a behavioral claim with a mechanical one.**
The paper's contribution 3 (introduction, paragraph 5) says Wald tests reject equality of first-LL and second-LL effects, and Section 6.1 (`robustness.tex`, ll. 4–9) frames this as evidence that "the initial access shock is the pivotal one." But equality could fail for at least two very different reasons: (a) genuine first-time pivotality — a behavioral or informational singularity in the first shock — versus (b) the mechanical diminishing returns story — repeat LL recipients already carry ranking capital from prior LL shocks, so the marginal ranking boost from another LL slot has less room to trigger threshold crossings. The mechanical decomposition (Section 6.2) documents the ratio but does not distinguish these interpretations. If (b) is the operative mechanism, the paper is really a paper about diminishing returns to opportunity in a threshold-based market, and the "first" framing is misleading. **Fix:** either (i) show that first-LL and second-LL players with comparable pre-treatment ranking-point levels have different effects (holding ranking capital constant), which would identify (a); or (ii) revise the framing to "diminishing marginal returns to opportunity when access is threshold-triggered" and drop the behavioral-first-break language. The current framing wants (a) but the evidence at hand supports (b).

**2. Pallais (2014) is the closest analog and is under-differentiated.**
The literature section (`literature.tex`, l. 6) mentions Pallais as "the closest labor market analog" but then moves on. Pallais also studies a randomized single opportunity in a labor market with reputational/rating feedback loops (oDesk ratings play the role tennis rankings play here), and she also finds persistent effects operating through the access channel. Reading the introduction, a reader could reasonably ask: "So what's new?" The differentiation points the paper *could* make — dynamic tracking at multiple horizons, separate measurement of access vs. ability via ranking-points vs. Elo, evidence of fade-out, first vs. second shock comparison — are all real but currently buried. **Fix:** rewrite the contribution paragraph so Pallais is the anchor, not Oyer/Oreopoulos, and articulate the 3–4 specific advances over Pallais (dynamic tracking, ability decomposition, cascade fade-out, marginal returns to $n$th shock). This is the single most consequential fix for the referee report.

**3. The $100K headline conflates a mechanical immediate-event prize with a causal access cascade.**
Section 5.3 (`mechanisms.tex`, l. 27) reports that a first-time Grand Slam LL recipient earns $100K–$400K in the main draw vs. a $40K qualifying stipend for controls, and adds an estimated $11K per additional main-draw entry over 26 weeks (evaluated at an ATT of extra draws). The immediate-event component is mechanical *conditional on advancement* — the treatment dose, not a causal cascade — while the extra-main-draws-over-26-weeks component is the causal cascade. The intro (paragraph 5) states "the total prize-money effect of a first-time Grand Slam LL entry is about $100,000 USD over the subsequent 26 weeks," which reads as if the whole $100K is the cascade. If, as I suspect, the immediate main-draw prize dominates the $100K figure, then the causal-cascade component is much smaller and the headline needs to be reframed. **Fix:** report the decomposition explicitly: $X of the $100K is immediate mechanical prize (i.e., what any player advancing to round $r$ earns), $Y of the $100K is the causal cascade over 26 weeks; only $Y is the object of interest for "opportunity shock" magnitude. Report both, do not merge them.

**4. The external-validity argument to "tournament labor markets" is asserted, not developed.**
JHR readers will ask: which tournament markets? Academic tenure? Corporate CEO promotion tournaments? VC funding rounds? Litigation? Each of these has distinctive features (long tenure clock, no rolling window, no continuous performance measure), and tennis's 52-week rolling window is precisely what makes fade-out happen. The paper actually leverages this in the conclusion (l. 8) — "market transparency as the key moderator" — which is the right analytical move, but the paper doesn't work through which other tournament markets share the rolling-window feature versus which don't. **Fix:** add a short paragraph in the discussion/conclusion (300–500 words) that categorizes tournament markets by (i) presence of continuous performance measurement and (ii) rolling reset windows, and identifies which markets should show fade-out (perhaps: some sales-force tournaments, quarterly bonus schemes) versus which should show persistent effects (tenure, junior-partner promotion). This is the JHR-facing add.

**5. The tournament-theory literature is missing.**
Given the paper's framing as "tournament labor markets" and its explicit engagement with initial-conditions and access-shock literatures, the omission of the tournament-theory canon is conspicuous: Lazear & Rosen (1981), Rosen (1986) on prizes in elimination tournaments, Prendergast (1999) survey on incentives. The paper cites Ehrenberg & Bognanno (1990) and Sunde (2009) in the sports-lab paragraph but does not engage them as tournament-theory papers. If the paper wants to speak to tournament labor markets as a class, it should engage the theoretical foundations, not just prior empirical sports-as-lab work. **Fix:** rewrite the literature review to have a distinct "tournament labor markets" paragraph that positions the paper within tournament theory (Lazear-Rosen), then situates access shocks within that framework. This is a real intellectual contribution the paper could make and currently does not.

**6. The WTA Elo interpretation flips between two positions.**
Section 5.1 (`mechanisms.tex`, l. 11) reports the WTA Elo gains and then discusses the match-level null: "the ability interpretation carries only limited weight ... Elo can improve even without a genuine ability change." But the intro (paragraph 5) and results (`results.tex`, l. 34) present the WTA Elo gains more assertively as evidence of "genuine ability improvements." I understand the honest hedging — the paper is trying to acknowledge tension — but the reader is left unsure which reading the authors endorse. **Fix:** commit to one interpretation. Given the match-level null, the cleaner story is that the WTA Elo gains reflect schedule composition (weaker opponents at lower-tier events) rather than skill improvement; then the paper's headline mechanism becomes "access on both tours, with the WTA schedule effect creating an Elo shadow of the access cascade." This is a stronger story than the current "modest ability gains on the WTA" reading.

## Minor Comments

1. Intro paragraph 4 reports the ATP 26-week point estimate as "72.6" with clustered $p = 0.047$ but Fisher $p = 0.164$. Given the paper's honest use of Fisher inference, the 26-week ATP effect should be described as "significant under clustered but not Fisher inference" in the abstract-adjacent claims, not lumped with 4–12 week estimates.
2. Section 5.2 (`mechanisms.tex`, l. 20) reports non-GS ATP $\hat{\delta} = +0.609$ ($p<0.001$) but does not reconcile the sign (positive) with the GS $\hat{\delta} = -0.141$ (negative). If the non-GS positive estimate reflects positive selection into treatment, that should be stated — the CF is supposed to correct for this, but the sign flip needs explaining.
3. The verified-lottery news check (Section 6.3, `robustness.tex`, l. 71) is a nice piece of investigative work. Consider putting a short summary of the 10 datable cases in an appendix table with tournament, year, withdrawing player, withdrawal date, and source. Referees like paper trails.
4. Section 3.4 (`data.tex`, l. 57) reports within-event $F$ balance for ATP as $p = 0.046$, driven by age. This is close enough to conventional rejection that saying "one marginal individual imbalance is consistent with chance" understates the referee concern. Report the point estimate of the age imbalance in years — the reader wants to know if we're comparing 23-year-olds to 25-year-olds or 22-year-olds to 27-year-olds.
5. The censoring rule (Section 4.3, `empirical_strategy.tex`, l. 47) is defensible, but 12 months is a long window; consider a robustness check with 6-month censoring given the paper's finding that effects fade by 52 weeks.
6. The heterogeneity result that effects are stronger for players aged $\geq 24$ (Section 5.3, `results.tex`, l. 91) is interpreted as "older players have more experience to convert a boost." An alternative reading is that older qualifying-level players are on their last chance and respond more sharply — a runway-shrinking mechanism. Both are consistent with the data; the paper should acknowledge both.
7. In the mechanisms section the phrase "access rather than ability" is repeatedly used but "ability" is operationalized only as Elo. A more careful phrasing is "measured skill as captured by Elo," since Elo has known limits (schedule sensitivity, K-factor choice).

## Missing Literature

- **Lazear & Rosen (1981)**, "Rank-Order Tournaments as Optimum Labor Contracts," JPE. The paper claims to speak to "tournament labor markets"; this is the founding paper of that literature.
- **Rosen (1986)**, "Prizes and Incentives in Elimination Tournaments," AER. On why elimination structure amplifies stakes late.
- **Prendergast (1999)**, "The Provision of Incentives in Firms," JEL. Standard survey; useful for positioning.
- **Ehrenberg & Bognanno (1990)**, JPE, "Do Tournaments Have Incentive Effects?" You cite the golf paper but not this JPE piece, which is the standard tournament-theory empirical citation.
- **Malmendier & Nagel (2011)**, "Depression Babies," QJE, is a first-shock-shapes-belief paper you should engage: it is a within-life exogenous shock affecting economic behavior, and it distinguishes belief formation from mechanical effects — exactly the kind of distinction Major Comment 1 asks you to make.
- **Kahn (2000)**, "The Sports Business as a Labor Market Laboratory," JEP. The standard citation for using sports as a labor-market lab; you need this for the JHR external-validity argument.
- **Genakos & Pagliero (2012)**, "Interim Rank, Risk Taking, and Performance in Dynamic Tournaments," JPE. Directly relevant to how rank position shapes competitive behavior in tournament markets.
- **Rosen (1981)**, "The Economics of Superstars," AER. If you're going to talk about ranking-based cascades, this is foundational.

## Questions for the Authors

1. Can you separate the "first shock is pivotal" story (behavioral/informational) from the "later strata have more ranking capital" story (mechanical diminishing returns)? Specifically: within the $n=1$ stratum, does the treatment effect vary with the ranking-point value of the prior LL award?
2. What is the decomposition of the $100K headline into (a) immediate main-draw prize conditional on advancement and (b) causal cascade from additional main-draw entries over 26 weeks? Is (a) mechanically much larger than (b)?
3. Is the WTA Elo finding driven by schedule composition (lower-tier opponents) or by within-match performance improvement? Can you condition on opponent Elo distribution to test this?
4. In the non-GS setting, why does the match-level $\hat{\delta}$ flip sign relative to the GS estimate? Is this an artifact of the control function or a real difference in the treated population?
5. The census of 10 news-verified rank-1 entries all turned out to be post-qualifying ranking assignments. Does this mean the ATP $F$-test $p = 0.106$ imbalance in the full first-time sample partially reflects that these 23 rank-1 entries are ranking-assigned? What happens to the balance test after excluding the news-verified 10?
6. Would you consider narrowing the target-journal framing to AEJ:Applied and letting go of JHR? The tennis-to-labor-policy translation is not obvious and might get sharper referees at JHR than the identification deserves.

## Verdict Paragraph

My top concern for the R&R response is the interpretive ambiguity in the "first break" framing (Major Comment 1). The paper wants to make a claim about the first shock being pivotal in a behavioral or informational sense, but the evidence it presents — the Wald test between $n=0$ and $n=1$ strata — is equally consistent with a mechanical story where later strata contain players who already carry ranking capital from prior shocks and therefore have less room to trigger threshold crossings. The mechanical decomposition (a nice addition since R3) documents the ratio but does not adjudicate between these interpretations. Either the paper must show — perhaps by conditioning on comparable pre-treatment ranking capital across strata — that first-time recipients respond differently for reasons other than their lower ranking baseline, or it must reframe the contribution as "diminishing marginal returns to opportunity in a threshold-based tournament market." Both are publishable; only one is what the current draft claims. Combined with the Pallais differentiation (Major Comment 2) and the $100K decomposition (Major Comment 3), addressing this concern would move the paper cleanly into Minor Revisions territory at AEJ:Applied. The paper is well-executed and honest about its limits; my scoring reflects real interpretive work still to be done, not doubts about the underlying research.
