\begin{abstract}
\noindent \singlespacing
Does a single randomly assigned tournament opportunity persistently shift a professional athlete's career trajectory? I study lucky losers in professional tennis---players who lose in qualifying but enter the main draw when another player withdraws. At Grand Slams, lucky losers are selected by lottery. In the full ATP Grand Slam sample ($N = 248$), a stacked specification shows that selection increases ranking points by 31.9 at 4 weeks ($p = 0.028$), 38.5 at 12 weeks ($p = 0.007$), and 49.2 at 26 weeks ($p = 0.054$). On the WTA tour ($N = 132$), effects are larger at short horizons---64.4 points at 4 weeks ($p = 0.003$) and 78.7 at 8 weeks ($p < 0.001$)---but fade by 26 weeks. Elo ratings are unchanged at every horizon on both tours. A match-level tournament performance model finds no significant effect on match win probability on either tour ($\hat{\delta} = -0.123$, $p = 0.119$; 8,543 ATP matches; $\hat{\delta} = +0.034$, $p = 0.760$; 5,391 WTA matches). Non-Grand-Slam estimates using a control function approach corroborate these patterns: ATP ranking point gains of 12.2 at 4 weeks ($p = 0.065$) and 28.1 at 26 weeks ($p = 0.009$). Access begets access, but not ability.
\end{abstract}
