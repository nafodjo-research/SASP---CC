\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

A randomly assigned tournament entry raises a marginal player's ranking for several months, and the gain operates through access to further tournaments rather than through improved play. In the first-time ATP Grand Slam sample ($N = 120$), lottery selection raises ranking points by 72.5 at 4 weeks, 72.1 at 8 weeks, and 82.8 at 12 weeks, each surviving Fisher randomization inference at 5{,}000 permutations ($p_{\text{Fisher}} = 0.006$, $0.022$, $0.025$). The 26-week estimate of 72.6 is significant under clustered inference ($p = 0.047$) but not under Fisher ($p_{\text{Fisher}} = 0.103$), and the effect is gone by 52 weeks. On the WTA tour ($N = 82$) the 8-week gain of 55.0 clears clustered but not randomization inference ($p = 0.017$ against $p_{\text{Fisher}} = 0.077$), so the WTA access result is the weaker of the two. Non-Grand-Slam control-function estimates on larger samples ($N = 2{,}054$ ATP; $N = 1{,}423$ WTA) reproduce the ATP access pattern, and these non-Grand-Slam access effects are the only results in the paper that survive family-wise error control across the full 80-test outcome set.

Two features of the design carry most of the weight in supporting a causal reading. Pre-treatment placebo regressions at $-12$, $-8$, and $-4$ weeks return coefficients indistinguishable from zero in all twelve tests across both tours and both the pooled and verified-lottery samples, which is what random assignment implies and what ranking-rule assignment would not produce. Lee (2009) bounds that correct for differential attrition are tight and exclude zero through 26 weeks, so the short- and medium-horizon results are not an artifact of treated players remaining observable at higher rates.

The mechanism is access accumulation. The ranking system does not adjust for opponent quality: a first-round Grand Slam win earns the same points regardless of who was beaten. Points earned at the focal event improve the player's position in the rolling ranking, which grants entry to subsequent tournaments and further chances to accumulate points. Elo, which does adjust for opponent quality, is flat on the ATP tour at every horizon, so the ranking gains reflect more draws entered and more matches played rather than better play. The evidence on the ATP tour also points weakly toward overseeding, in the sense that a player whose ranking runs ahead of their ability draws stronger opponents: the match-level estimate is negative but imprecise ($\hat\delta = -0.141$, $p = 0.17$), while the verified-lottery subsample shows Elo declining at 12 and 52 weeks. The WTA evidence differs, with Elo rising at several horizons in a pattern that a schedule-composition account can also generate.

The return to an opportunity does decline with the number already received, but not between the first and the second. In a saturated specification estimated on the pooled sample, the second-LL shifter is statistically indistinguishable from zero at every horizon, while the third-plus shifter is large, negative, and significant throughout, and a joint test rejects a flat profile on three of four samples. Returns are stable across the first two opportunities and collapse at the third, which is what a threshold account predicts when the direct-entry cutoff sits above what a single main-draw appearance delivers.

The self-limiting character of the cascade is the finding that travels furthest. Where \citet{Oyer2006_initial_placement} and \citet{Oreopoulos2012_graduating_recession} document effects of initial conditions that run for a decade, the access gain here is gone inside a year. The difference is not the size of the shock but the memory of the market. Tennis rankings are a rolling 52-week window: the points that generated the advantage are mechanically retired on a known schedule, and sustained performance is the only thing that replaces them. That suggests a way to classify tournament labor markets by how long an access shock should survive in each. Markets that continuously measure performance and periodically reset the record, such as sales tournaments with quarterly quotas or ranked freelance platforms with recency-weighted ratings, should show decay on the timescale of the reset. Markets that measure performance coarsely and record it permanently, such as academic tenure clocks, partnership tracks, and other up-or-out promotion ladders, have no comparable retirement mechanism, and an early access shock there should persist in the way the initial-conditions literature finds. \citet{Pallais2014_inefficient_hiring} sits at the persistent end: an oDesk rating, once earned, does not expire. The prediction is testable wherever a market's record-keeping horizon can be measured, and it implies that the durability of an opportunity shock is a design parameter of the market rather than a fact about workers.

The economic magnitudes separate into two quantities that should not be added casually. A Grand Slam main-draw slot pays about \$94{,}000 more than a qualifying exit, which is one to two years of a qualifying-level player's typical earnings and explains why these slots matter so much to the players who receive them. That figure is mechanical: it is what the tournament pays for the slot. The causal cascade this paper identifies, the additional main draws entered over the following 26 weeks, is worth roughly \$7{,}300, about 8 percent of the immediate windfall. On non-Grand-Slam events the ordering reverses and the cascade dominates.

Three limitations bound the results. The Grand Slam lottery samples are small, and the verified-lottery subsample that restricts to provably randomized events is smaller still ($N = 64$ ATP, $N = 41$ WTA), so nulls on that subsample cannot separate a zero effect from an effect the sample lacks the power to detect. Lottery contamination remains partially unresolved: withdrawal timing is not in the public record, two data-only criteria certify 44 of 64 events as lotteries, and a news audit of a subset of the remainder dated ten entries, all to post-qualifying withdrawals that trigger the ranking rule. Finally, the 52-week fade-out is the one result that differential attrition can also explain, since treated players remain in the rankings at higher rates at that horizon and the Lee bounds widen to include zero. Longer-run outcomes, and settings where the market's record-keeping horizon differs, are the natural next tests.
