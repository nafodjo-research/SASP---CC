\section{Mechanisms}
\label{sec:mechanisms}

The results establish that lucky loser entry causes gains in ranking points and tournament access. This section decomposes the effect into two channels and examines match-level performance conditional on opponent strength. Throughout, ability means measured skill as captured by Elo: a rating that updates after each match according to the outcome and the two players' prior ratings. Elo is sensitive to the schedule of opponents faced and to the choice of $K$-factor, so it proxies for playing strength rather than measuring it directly, and Section~\ref{sec:mechanisms_access} tests the schedule-sensitivity concern rather than assuming it away.

\subsection{Access versus Measured Skill}
\label{sec:mechanisms_access}

The career effects of LL entry operate primarily through tournament access rather than improved measured skill. On the ATP tour, ranking points rise significantly through 26 weeks while Elo ratings are null at every horizon. Because Elo adjusts for opponent quality, the null Elo result indicates that treated players do not perform better or worse per match once opponent strength is held constant. The ranking-point gains come entirely from additional tournament participation: LL entry provides a main-draw appearance that generates ranking points, which improve the player's position in the rolling 52-week ranking, which crosses direct-entry thresholds for subsequent lower-tier events. The evidence aligns with this access cascade: treated players enter more main draws and play more matches at 250+ events over 26 weeks, and their quality-adjusted match performance is unchanged.

The WTA tour behaves differently. Elo rises at 8 weeks ($+15.0$, $p = 0.082$) and 52 weeks ($+36.8$, $p = 0.055$) in the pooled Grand Slam sample and at 12 weeks in the non-Grand-Slam sample ($+10.5$, $p = 0.011$). On the verified-lottery subsample the point estimates keep the same sign and comparable magnitude at short horizons ($+12.7$ at 4 weeks, $+22.1$ at 8 weeks) but none reaches significance, which is what the minimum detectable effects for a 41-observation subsample imply. The evidence for a WTA ability channel therefore rests on the pooled and non-Grand-Slam samples, and the verified subsample neither strengthens nor undermines it.

Elo can rise for a reason unrelated to ability, because it rewards wins against any opponent: if the ranking gain routes treated players into lower-tier events with weaker fields, they win more often and Elo increases without any change in how well they play. This schedule-composition account has a direct empirical implication, namely that treated players should face measurably weaker opponents after the focal event. Table~\ref{tab:schedule} tests it by computing, for every match a player contests in the 26 weeks following the event, the opponent's pre-match Elo, and regressing the player's mean opponent Elo on treatment.

The account does not survive the test. Treated WTA players face opponents who are 22.9 Elo points \textit{stronger} in the pooled sample and 33.9 points stronger on the verified subsample, and while neither difference is statistically significant ($p = 0.121$ and $p = 0.137$), both point estimates run in the direction opposite to the one the schedule story requires. Win rates are also higher rather than lower ($+0.037$ pooled, $+0.047$ verified). Facing stronger opposition and winning more of those matches is what improved play looks like, and it is not what an easier draw looks like. The ability interpretation therefore survives the most direct test available, though the individual estimates remain imprecise and the match-level model does not independently confirm it ($\hat{\delta} = +0.146$, $p = 0.29$).

On the ATP tour the same test returns a different pattern that fits the access-only reading: opponent strength is unchanged ($+22.4$, $p = 0.161$ pooled; $-9.7$, $p = 0.644$ verified) while win rates are flat to slightly lower ($-0.017$ and $-0.051$). Treated ATP players play a similar schedule and do not win more of it, which is what a pure access effect implies.

The access cascade is self-limiting. As the 52-week rolling window progresses, the points from the original LL event constitute a shrinking fraction of the player's total. Unless downstream tournaments generate enough additional points to replace the original ones, the ranking converges back toward the level determined by sustained match performance. The dynamic results align with this: ATP ranking-point gains peak at 12 weeks and fade toward zero at 52 weeks.

\subsection{Match-Level Performance}
\label{sec:mechanisms_performance}

To test directly whether LL entry affects competitive performance itself, I estimate a match-level logit of win probability conditional on opponent strength and tournament context (see Appendix~\ref{app:match_model}). At Grand Slams, the lottery provides exogenous variation; at non-GS events, a control function corrects for endogenous selection.

First-time ATP GS LL recipients have $\hat{\delta} = -0.141$ (SE $= 0.103$, $p = 0.17$; player-clustered), a point estimate consistent with overseeding when novice entrants face opponents above their ability level, though not significant at conventional levels. WTA GS recipients have $\hat{\delta} = +0.146$ (SE $= 0.138$, $p = 0.29$), also not statistically distinguishable from zero. The GS point estimates line up with the direction the overseeding channel predicts, negative for ATP and positive for WTA, but the small Grand Slam samples do not deliver enough power for individually significant tests.

The non-Grand-Slam ATP estimate is $\hat{\delta} = +0.609$ ($p < 0.001$), which reverses the Grand Slam sign and deserves comment rather than assimilation. Three differences separate the two settings. Selection is the first: non-Grand-Slam slots go to the highest-ranked qualifying losers by rule, so treated players there are positively selected on precisely the ability the outcome measures, and the control function corrects for that selection only under the joint-normality assumption that Section~\ref{sec:robustness_cf} shows fails on the ATP randomized benchmark. The estimate is accordingly better read as an upper bound than as a comparable treatment effect. The second is the opponent distribution: a non-Grand-Slam main draw at the 250 or 500 level places a qualifying-margin player against opponents far closer to their own level than a Grand Slam draw does, so the overseeding channel that pushes the Grand Slam estimate negative is weak or absent. The third is composition: the non-Grand-Slam sample is seventeen times larger and spans a wider range of tournament tiers. Given the failed validation, I do not treat the positive non-Grand-Slam estimate as evidence against the Grand Slam overseeding pattern.

Full results including dose and horizon interactions are reported in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:delta_all}.

The event study figures (Figures~\ref{fig:event_study_atp} and~\ref{fig:event_study_wta}) provide a visual summary: ranking-point trajectories diverge sharply after the LL event while pre-event trends are parallel, so the treatment effect is concentrated in post-event access gains rather than pre-existing ability differences.

\subsection{Economic Magnitude: Prize Money}
\label{sec:mechanisms_prize}

Ranking points are an imperfect proxy for the economic stakes. To translate the results into dollars I use publicly available tournament prize tables, and I keep two conceptually distinct quantities separate throughout, because they answer different questions and differ by an order of magnitude.

The first quantity is the \textit{immediate prize}, which is the dollar value of the treatment dose itself. A Grand Slam main-draw entrant earns between \$100{,}000 and \$400{,}000 depending on the round reached, against a roughly \$40{,}000 stipend for a final-round qualifying loser. Averaged over the observed distribution of rounds reached, LL entry raises focal-event earnings by \$93{,}800 on the ATP tour and \$86{,}700 on the WTA tour. This is mechanical: it is what the tournament pays for occupying a main-draw slot, conditional on advancing as far as the player did. It is not a causal cascade, and it would accrue to any player placed in that slot.

The second quantity is the \textit{access cascade}, which is the causal object this paper estimates. Treated players enter roughly 0.66 additional main draws over the following 26 weeks; valued at an average forward-looking first-round prize of \$11{,}000, that is \$7{,}300 in downstream earnings on both Grand Slam samples. On the non-Grand-Slam samples, where the immediate prize is far smaller, the cascade dominates the total: \$18{,}800 of the \$27{,}800 ATP effect and \$10{,}200 of the \$16{,}200 WTA effect come from downstream access rather than the focal event. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:prize} reports the two components and their sum for all four samples.

The comparison is informative in both directions. A Grand Slam LL slot is worth about \$94{,}000 on the spot, roughly one to two years of a qualifying-level player's typical \$40{,}000--\$80{,}000 annual earnings, which is why these slots matter enormously to the players who receive them. The downstream cascade adds a further \$7{,}300 over 26 weeks, about 8 percent of the immediate windfall. Under a $\pm 20\%$ band on the per-entry prize assumption (\$8{,}800--\$13{,}200 rather than \$11{,}000) the cascade component ranges from \$5{,}800 to \$8{,}700, and from \$15{,}000 to \$22{,}600 on the non-GS ATP sample. The ranking-point cascade this paper identifies is therefore economically real but modest next to the direct payment for the slot, and it fades within the year.

\subsection{Reconciling with the Existing Literature}
\label{sec:mechanisms_reconciling}

\citet{Maity2025_early_career_sports} report positive correlations between lucky loser entry and career outcomes. The match-level tournament performance model, which exploits the lottery for identification, does not detect an improvement in match-level win probability on the ATP tour and reports only a marginal positive effect on the WTA tour. The causal estimates here indicate that the correlational finding in \citet{Maity2025_early_career_sports} likely reflects the additional tournament participation that LL entry generates rather than an improvement in competitive performance itself.

The initial conditions literature reports persistent effects of early-career shocks \citep{Oyer2006_initial_placement, Oreopoulos2012_graduating_recession}. The present results align on the access dimension: LL entry causes persistent changes in the number and type of tournaments a player enters; the ability dimension is largely null. Market transparency is the plausible moderator: in professional tennis, where performance is measured match by match and rankings update weekly, ability reasserts itself within a year. The access channel alone accounts for the ranking-point gains and tournament-entry effects documented in Section~\ref{sec:results}.
