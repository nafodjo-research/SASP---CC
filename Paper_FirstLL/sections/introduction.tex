\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

In the summer of 2019, Brayden Schnur lost in the final round of Wimbledon qualifying and was, by the ordinary operation of the draw, finished for the fortnight. Borna Coric then withdrew from the main draw with a back injury, and Schnur was placed into the vacancy as a lucky loser. He played Wimbledon; the players he had been level with three days earlier did not. Professional tennis produces this natural experiment several times a year, and at Grand Slam events it produces it by literal lottery.

The question this makes answerable is what an opportunity is worth in a labor market where opportunity is rationed by rank. Tournament labor markets in the sense of \citet{Lazear1981_tournaments} pay for relative rather than absolute performance, and the theoretical literature on them studies effort within a contest whose entrants are taken as given. Tennis runs a sequence of contests in which rank determines entry and entry determines the chance to earn rank, so a shock to one propagates into the other. A player granted a main-draw slot earns points, the points improve their ranking, and the improved ranking admits them to tournaments they would have had to qualify for. Whether that feedback loop converts a single administrative accident into a lasting career difference is an empirical question about how long the market's memory is.

I study lucky losers on the ATP and WTA tours: players who lose in the final round of qualifying and enter the main draw when an accepted player withdraws. At Grand Slams the vacancy is filled by a random draw among the top four ranked qualifying losers when the withdrawal precedes the end of qualifying, which supplies the randomization. I restrict the analysis to players who have never previously received a lucky loser slot, so treated and control players alike enter the pool with no prior exposure to this form of access. Two features of the setting support a decomposition that is rarely available. Ranking points reward participation without adjusting for the quality of the opponent; Elo ratings adjust for it. Tracking both after the same randomized shock separates a pure access effect from an improvement in play.

The empirical strategy has three components. Grand Slam lottery assignment provides the primary identification, with outcomes measured at horizons $h \in \{4, 8, 12, 26, 52\}$ weeks and event fixed effects restricting comparisons to within an event's top-four pool. A control-function approach extends the analysis to non-Grand-Slam events, where selection follows a ranking rule: the generalized residual is built from the exact enumeration probability that a player's rank among qualifying losers falls within the available slots, computed over the full combinatorial structure of the qualifying draw. A match-level model then tests whether entry changes win probability conditional on opponent strength.

Lottery selection raises ranking points by 72.5 at 4 weeks, 72.1 at 8 weeks, and 82.8 at 12 weeks in the first-time ATP Grand Slam sample ($N = 120$), and each survives Fisher randomization inference at 5{,}000 permutations ($p_{\text{Fisher}} = 0.006$, $0.022$, $0.025$). The 26-week estimate of 72.6 clears the threshold with clustered standard errors ($p = 0.047$) but under neither the wild cluster bootstrap ($p_{\text{boot}} = 0.052$) nor Fisher inference ($p_{\text{Fisher}} = 0.103$), and the effect is indistinguishable from zero at 52 weeks. On the WTA tour ($N = 82$) the estimated gain concentrates at 8 weeks ($+55.0$), though it is significant only under clustered inference ($p = 0.017$) and not under randomization inference ($p_{\text{Fisher}} = 0.077$); Elo improves at medium horizons on that tour. Non-Grand-Slam control-function estimates on larger samples ($N = 2{,}054$ ATP; $N = 1{,}423$ WTA) reproduce the ATP pattern. Two checks carry most of the weight in supporting a causal reading: pre-treatment placebo regressions at $-12$, $-8$, and $-4$ weeks are indistinguishable from zero in all twelve tests, and Lee bounds correcting for differential attrition are tight and stay positive through 26 weeks on the ATP tour.

The evidence is not uniform in strength, and Section~\ref{sec:robustness_mht} sets out the ranking explicitly. The non-Grand-Slam access effects survive Romano-Wolf family-wise error control across the full 80-test outcome set and are the most secure results in the paper. The Grand Slam ranking-point effects survive randomization inference and false-discovery-rate correction but not family-wise control over that set, which is a demanding standard for a design with 120 observations and which treats a dynamic path across horizons as five independent claims rather than one. I report both standards throughout rather than the more favourable one.

This paper makes four contributions. First, it provides causal estimates of a within-career opportunity shock in a tournament labor market, with a decomposition into access and ability that the closest existing design cannot deliver. \citet{Pallais2014_inefficient_hiring} randomizes first job opportunities on an online labor market and finds persistent employment gains transmitted through a public rating, which is the same mechanism a tennis ranking implements. The advances here are that outcomes are observed at five horizons rather than one, so the trajectory is identified rather than assumed; that the market reports two distinct measures of a worker, which separates access from ability; that the effect is observed to decay, at a rate the market's rolling window determines; and that some players receive the same randomized shock more than once, which identifies how the return varies with the number already received.

Second, the access-versus-ability decomposition finds that ranking gains on the ATP tour come from more draws entered and more matches played rather than from better play: Elo is flat at every horizon. The evidence points weakly toward overseeding, in which a player whose ranking runs ahead of their ability meets stronger opponents, but it remains suggestive: the match-level estimate is negative and imprecise ($\hat\delta = -0.141$, player-clustered $p = 0.17$), and the verified-lottery subsample shows an Elo decline only at 52 weeks ($-51.3$, $p = 0.074$). The WTA tour behaves differently, with Elo rising at several horizons in the pooled sample; a direct test rules out the most obvious alternative explanation, since treated WTA players face stronger rather than weaker opponents after the focal event.

Third, the return to an opportunity declines with the number already received, but not where an emphasis on the first shock would predict. In a saturated specification estimated on the pooled sample, the second-lucky-loser shifter is statistically indistinguishable from zero at every horizon, while the third-plus shifter is large, negative, and significant throughout; a joint test rejects a flat experience profile on three of four samples. Returns are stable across the first two opportunities and collapse at the third, consistent with a direct-entry threshold that a single main-draw appearance is often insufficient to clear.

Fourth, the results separate two economic magnitudes that are easily conflated. A Grand Slam main-draw slot pays about \$94{,}000 more than a qualifying exit, which is one to two years of a qualifying-level player's typical earnings; this is what the tournament pays for the slot and is mechanical given the round reached. The causal cascade, the additional main draws entered over the following 26 weeks, is worth roughly \$7{,}300, about 8 percent of that immediate figure. On non-Grand-Slam events, where the immediate prize is far smaller, the ordering reverses.

The access gain fades within a year, which contrasts with the decade-long persistence documented for initial labor market conditions \citep{Oyer2006_initial_placement, Oreopoulos2012_graduating_recession}. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} argues that the difference lies in the market's record-keeping horizon rather than in the size of the shock: the 52-week rolling ranking window mechanically retires the points that created the advantage, whereas a permanent record has no comparable expiry.
