\begin{abstract}
\noindent \singlespacing
Does a worker's \textit{first} randomly assigned opportunity shape careers more than subsequent ones? I study lucky losers in professional tennis: players who lose in qualifying but enter the main draw when another player withdraws. Restricting the sample to players never previously granted a lucky-loser slot isolates the effect of a first-ever access shock. Grand Slam allocation is randomized among the top four qualifying losers, giving a lottery design ($N = 120$ ATP, $N = 82$ WTA). In the ATP first-time sample, ranking-point gains reach 72.5 points at 4 weeks and 82.8 at 12 weeks (Fisher $p < 0.001$ and $p = 0.028$); a 26-week effect of 72.6 points is significant with clustered inference ($p = 0.047$) but not under Fisher randomization ($p = 0.164$), and effects fade by 52 weeks. On the WTA tour, ranking-point gains concentrate at 8 weeks ($+55.0$; $p = 0.017$), with Elo also improving at medium horizons. A non-Grand-Slam control-function analysis on larger samples ($N = 2{,}054$ ATP; $N = 1{,}423$ WTA) reports the same access pattern. Comparing marginal effects of successive lucky-loser entries, Wald tests reject equality of first- and second-LL effects for non-GS tournament access ($p = 0.002$ ATP; $p = 0.009$ WTA); a mechanical decomposition of the pooled effect into stratum-weighted components documents that the ratio $\hat\beta^{(n=0)} / \hat\beta^{\text{pool}}$ is roughly two on the ATP tour. The implied prize-money effect of a first-time Grand Slam LL entry is about \$100{,}000, comparable to a qualifying-level player's annual earnings. The initial access shock is the pivotal one in this transparent tournament market; the ranking cascade dissipates within a year as the 52-week rolling window absorbs new performance signals.
\end{abstract}
