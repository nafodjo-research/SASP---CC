\begin{abstract}
\noindent \singlespacing
Does a worker's \textit{first} randomly assigned opportunity shape careers more than subsequent ones? I study lucky losers in professional tennis, qualifying-round losers who enter the main draw when another player withdraws, and restrict the analysis to first-time recipients so that both treated and control players are LL-naive. Grand Slam allocation is randomized among the top four qualifying losers ($N = 120$ ATP, $N = 82$ WTA); a non-Grand-Slam control-function analysis extends the design to larger samples ($N = 2{,}054$ ATP; $N = 1{,}423$ WTA). Ranking-point gains peak at 12 weeks on the ATP tour ($+82.8$; Fisher $p = 0.028$) and at 8 weeks on the WTA tour ($+55.0$; $p = 0.017$), imply about \$100{,}000 in prize money over the subsequent 26 weeks, and fade by 52 weeks as the rolling ranking window absorbs new performance signals. On the ATP tour the mechanism is access rather than ability (Elo is unchanged at every horizon); the WTA tour shows modest Elo gains that align with an additional ability channel, and marginal effects diminish with LL experience (Wald tests reject equality of first- and second-LL effects on non-GS access; ATP $p = 0.002$; WTA $p = 0.009$). A verified-lottery subsample built from a data-only criterion supports the ATP 26-week effect ($p = 0.050$) and the WTA 8-week effect ($p = 0.005$), so ranking-based contamination is not driving the headline result.
\end{abstract}
