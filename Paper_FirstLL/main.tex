\begin{abstract}
\noindent \singlespacing
What is a randomly assigned opportunity worth in a labor market where access is rationed by rank? I study lucky losers in professional tennis, players who lose in final-round qualifying and enter the main draw when an accepted player withdraws, exploiting the lottery that Grand Slams use to fill such vacancies among the top four qualifying losers ($N = 120$ ATP, $N = 82$ WTA; a control-function design extends the analysis to $N = 2{,}054$ ATP and $N = 1{,}423$ WTA non-Grand-Slam events). Entry raises ranking points by 72 to 83 through 12 weeks on the ATP tour, surviving randomization inference, with a weaker 8-week gain of 55 points on the WTA tour and no effect on either tour within a year. Twelve pre-treatment placebo tests are indistinguishable from zero, and Lee bounds correcting for differential attrition stay positive through 26 weeks on the ATP tour. The gain operates through access rather than play: Elo, which adjusts for opponent quality, is flat on the ATP tour at every horizon, so the ranking gain reflects more draws entered rather than better play. Returns are stable across the first two opportunities and collapse at the third, so the decline in the return to opportunity is real but arrives later than a first-mover account implies. A Grand Slam slot pays about \$94{,}000 directly, while the downstream access cascade it triggers is worth roughly \$7{,}300. Persistence appears to depend on the market's record-keeping horizon: a rolling 52-week ranking window retires the initial shock on a known schedule, unlike the permanent records in settings where initial-condition effects run for a decade.
\end{abstract}
